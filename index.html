Hide, Erase, or Replace?

An Empirical Study of Cross-Gripper Policy Transfer

Jack Goler, Austin Patel, Chuer Pan, Shuran Song

Paper arXiv Video Rollouts Code

Why this study

Handheld interfaces like UMI already abstract the robot away: the camera is mounted on the end effector, so the data depends on neither the arm nor its kinematics. One piece of the embodiment is still in every frame: the gripper. Suppose you were to collect data for a policy on one end-effector. How could you deploy it on another? Swap it and the observations shift exactly where the policy has learned to look, and re-collecting demonstrations for every new gripper is not practical. Three families of remedies exist: hide the end effector from the model's attention, erase it from the image, or replace it with the target gripper. We compare all three against the unmodified-data control under matched conditions, on six manipulation experiments across five end effectors. We conduct an empirical study to see which works.

How should we handle the visual gap to deploy wrist-camera policies on a new end effector?

Which visual adaptation strategy transfers best to a new gripper?

Four things to take away

1
Current visual adaptation strategies hurt more than they help.
Hiding, erasing or replacing the fingers never beat the untouched source data on any of our tasks, zero-shot or after finetuning. Narrowing the visual gap costs the contact cues the policy relies on, so skip the preprocessing.
2
Finetuning eliminates the need for visual adaptation.
Pretrain on the source data as it is, collect about 25 demonstrations on the new end-effector, and finetune. Across all tasks, finetuning on top of the unadapted source demonstrations largely recovers the source data's value.
3
Do not count on zero-shot across a camera or embodiment change.
Swapping only the camera on an identical gripper, or only the hand under the same camera, drops zero-shot success to near zero. Budget for those target demos from the start.
4
Bridging a visual gap is easier than bridging a contact gap.
Finetuning on 25 demos closes the mismatch between different end-effectors during training and deployment. Differences in how the source gripper made contact with the object, artifacts, altered contact information, or other consequences of modifying demos are harder to fix through finetuning.

The surprising finding? Leaving the source data untouched transfers best, zero-shot and after finetuning. Methods that edit the wrist view narrow the visual gap but destroy the fine contact cues the policy needs, and that costs more than it returns.

QuestionDoes editing the wrist view help a policy transfer to a new gripper?

Cup saucer: initial study

Prehensile and non-prehensile contact
Source+
→
Target

No. Untouched source data transfers best: 53% zero-shot against 27% for the best adapted version, and every method reaches the oracle after 25 target demos.

Place a cup on a saucer with its handle facing left. When the handle points right, the policy must first rotate the cup with the outer surface of the open gripper, then grasp and place it. Source data: 592 demonstrations (290 Default UMI, 302 UMI-FT); target UMI-Air.

Zero-shot, the unmodified source policy reaches 53% against a 93% oracle. The adapted strategies were expected to close the visual gap but widened it: hide 7%, replace 27%, erase 17%. With 25 target demonstrations every method reaches the oracle, using 5% of the oracle's data; 25 demos alone reach only 60%.

Given every method reaches oracle performance after finetuning, we seek an experiment that differentiates the effects of finetuning on each of the adaptation strategies. We expect such a separation to arise in a task that depends on precise outer-finger non-prehensile contact; this motivates our next experiment.

Takeaways
  • Editing the fingers out of the wrist view makes zero-shot transfer worse, not better.
  • With a handful of target demos, every strategy catches up to the oracle.
  • Source data helps during finetuning: finetune demos alone fall well short.

Push-T: non-prehensile study

Non-prehensile contact
Source+
→
Target

Yes. The gap that cup saucer could not show opens here: after finetuning, no adaptation reaches 77% while replace stays at 50%. Misleading contact cues are harder to unlearn than the visual gap they were meant to close.

Push a T-shaped block into a printed T outline using only the outside of the open fingers, aligning orientation first, then sliding. Success requires no point of the block more than 1.5 cm outside the print. Source data: 1,572 demonstrations (770 Default UMI, 802 UMI-FT); target UMI-Air. Harder than third-person Push-T, since the wrist camera sees local contact but little of the block's global pose.

No adaptation again gives the best zero-shot performance (43%). Unlike cup saucer, the methods now separate after finetuning, which is what lets us isolate their differences: no adaptation, where the finetuning data only has to bridge the visual gap and minor contact differences, reaches 77%, while replace, which closes the visual gap but corrupts the contact information during pretraining, reaches 50%. The replaced fingers show no real deformation, shadow or position relative to the block. The from-scratch control is 3%, so source data matters more here than on cup saucer, where 25 demos alone reached 60%.

Takeaways
  • Here, the effects of the strategies are differentiated after finetuning.
  • Untouched source data performs best in both the zero-shot and finetuned regimes.
  • No-adaptation working best suggests that it is easier to bridge the visual gap through finetuning than the gap introduced by poor contact information.

Open a drawer by its handle. Default UMI and UMI-FT are too thick to enter the handle, so they pinch it and pull; UMI-Air fits inside and hooks downward. We transfer to UMI-FT, which must follow the Default UMI strategy, and ablate whether the conflicting UMI-Air demonstrations are included in pretraining (78 Default-only vs 181 total).

Filtering out the conflicting strategy hurts. With the full source, UMI-FT mostly took the compatible pinch strategy anyway and reached further toward the handle; after finetuning it always pinched, and no adaptation reached 97%.

Two conflicting strategies in the source data
Takeaways
  • Keeping the conflicting UMI-Air demonstrations in the source helps rather than hurts, zero-shot and after finetuning.
  • Extra embodiments in the pretraining data reduce overfitting to the source gripper, even when their strategy does not apply to the target.
  • Once again, no adaptation strategy improves on unmodified source data.

Not zero-shot, and only modestly after finetuning. Zero-shot is 0%; 25 iPhUMI demos reach 53% against 47% from scratch, about what the source policy manages on its own gripper.

Pick up a cup of any type in any orientation and place it on a box lid. A no-adaptation policy trained on 765 GenRobot demonstrations (stereo cameras, different fingers) is deployed on the iPhUMI gripper with Default UMI fingers, zero-shot and after finetuning on 25 iPhUMI demonstrations.

Zero-shot is 0%. Finetuning reaches 53% against 47% from scratch, a marginal gain; the same pretrained policy on its own GenRobot gripper also reaches only 53%, so finetuning may have recovered most of the source data's value.

Takeaways
  • When the camera and gripper change together, zero-shot transfer fails completely.
  • Finetuning recovers the source policy’s performance, but no more: the pretrained policy was the ceiling.
  • The gain over training from scratch is modest here because the source policy was itself limited.
QuestionWhat prevents zero-shot transfer: the camera or the embodiment?

Either one is enough to break it. Two experiments change exactly one factor each: the camera on an identical gripper, or the end effector under the same camera. Both drive zero-shot success to near zero, and 25 target demos recover most of the source data's value in both.

7% zero-shot, 87% after 25 demos (oracle 93%). Same gripper, same scene; only the lens changes.

To separate the camera from the gripper, we return to cup saucer and hold the end effector fixed (iPhUMI with Default UMI fingers), recording source demonstrations with a GoPro in the UMI camera mount and deploying with the iPhone. The fisheye GoPro and rectilinear iPhone differ in field of view, distortion, and where the fingers fall in frame. With an identical end effector there is nothing to hide, erase, or replace, so only no adaptation is evaluated.

Zero-shot, the GoPro-trained policy succeeds in 7% of trials on the iPhone. After finetuning on 25 iPhone demonstrations it reaches 87%, against a 93% oracle.

Takeaways
  • A camera change alone, with the end-effector hardware otherwise held fixed, is enough to break zero-shot transfer.
  • Finetuning on a few target camera demos recovers the source policy’s full performance, close to the oracle.
  • The source data still carries most of the value: the same demos from scratch fall well short.

0% zero-shot for every strategy, 83% after 25 demos (10% from scratch). Same camera, different hand.

Pick a drink carton from a table and place it in a recycling bin. Source demonstrations are collected by a human hand wearing a wrist-mounted iPhone (142 demonstrations); the target is an XHand dexterous hand with the same camera and a mount designed to align the wrist pose. All four strategies are evaluated with the human-hand variant of the replacement pipeline.

Zero-shot, every condition is 0%. After finetuning on 25 XHand demonstrations, no adaptation again performs best at 83%; training from scratch reaches 10%.

Takeaways
  • A large morphology gap alone is too large for any strategy to cross zero-shot, even when the camera is the same between training and deployment.
  • With a few XHand demos, finetuning on unmodified human-hand data works well, far beyond training from scratch.
  • Once again, the adaptation strategies all trail no adaptation, suggesting that even for large embodiment gaps no adaptation works best.

What we take away

Demonstrations collected on the wrong end effector are a pretraining resource, not a liability: leave them untouched, add a few target demonstrations, and the policy transfers. Today's visual adaptation methods cost more than they return.

Questions & Answers

If no adaptation wins, why study hide, erase, and replace at all?

Each prior method was introduced on a task where it helped, but none had been compared against the plain control under matched conditions across tasks and embodiments. The point of the study is that comparison. A practitioner with a dataset on one end effector and a robot with another now has a baseline to beat before paying for a transformation pipeline.

Why does editing the wrist image hurt?

The region the methods remove or re-render is exactly where the policy learns to look: the finger-object boundary. Finger deformation, shadows, and the relative position of finger and object tell the policy whether contact is happening and where. Hiding and erasing delete that signal; replacement draws fingers that were never actually touching the object. The visual gap shrinks, but the contact signal is gone.

How do you handle different finger lengths between grippers?

We move each gripper's tool-center-point to its fingertip and express relative actions in the source frame during training, then command the target's own TCP at deployment. On the drawer task this choice matters: deploying zero-shot on UMI-FT with its own TCP succeeds in 73% of trials, against 57% commanded to the Default UMI TCP and 50% commanded to the UMI-Air TCP.

How much does 25 target demonstrations buy?

A lot, as long as there is source data behind it. On cup saucer, 25 demos alone reach 60%; with 592 source demos on other grippers they reach the 93% oracle. On drawer and recycling the from-scratch control is 3% and 10%, while the finetuned no-adaptation policy reaches 97% and 83%.

Would adaptation help at a larger scale?

Possibly, but our results argue against expecting it. If a transformation does not help in the low-diversity regime we study, where the visual gap dominates, there is little reason to believe it becomes useful once embodiment diversity in the data grows and visual alignment matters less. We leave action-space representation and larger-scale mixing to future work.
